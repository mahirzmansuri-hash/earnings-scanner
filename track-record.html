Track record

Full write-up: research paper (PDF). Stories behind the numbers: case studies (Sears, Bed Bath & Beyond, GE, Kraft Heinz, SVB) and Receipts.

Every number here comes from a test where the model was trained only on years whose outcomes were already known, then scored years it had never seen (2016-2023). Companies that later failed or stopped filing are included. Banks, insurers and other financial companies are left out because these scores were not built for them.

At the same share of companies flagged as Altman's distress zone (50%), Faultline caught 92.4% of bankruptcies against Altman's 85.9%. To catch as many as Altman, Faultline needed to flag 35% of companies instead of 50%.

Adding auditor changes and late filings barely moved the result. Those events do carry warning signs on their own, but the financial statements already contain most of that information.

What happened to the stock

Free prices for companies that no longer trade are not available, so this uses the market value of outside shareholders' stock reported on each 10-K cover page. The change is measured over a year that starts after the score was public. Bankruptcies count as -100%, companies that stopped filing without being bought as -50%, and buyouts are left out. The typical flagged company lost value, and flagged companies were about three times as likely to lose half of it. The average was not lower, because a few flagged stocks rose enormously and new share issues raise market value. This is an approximation, not a trading test.

Quarterly updates

A 10-K comes once a year; a 10-Q comes every quarter. At each of 99,584 10-Q filing dates (2016-2023), a model fed the 10-Q's balance sheet and trailing-twelve-month results ranked bankruptcies within two years with an AUC of 0.825, against 0.808 for the score from the last 10-K. Its riskiest 10% held 51% of them, against 43%. The gain grows as the 10-K gets older (0.86 vs 0.85 when it is more than eight months old). Of 464 companies that went bankrupt, the 10-Q model placed 324 in the riskiest 10% at some point, against 263, a median of 512 days before the filing against 507. Company pages show the 10-Q update when it is newer than the 10-K.

Accounting fraud

The SEC names companies in Accounting and Auditing Enforcement Releases, usually years after the fact (a median of six years after the first misstated year). The test covers 95 companies' cases, 266 misstated company-years (2013-2019). A model that only knew the cases announced so far reached an AUC of 0.63 and its riskiest 10% held 24% of fraud years. The Beneish M-Score reached 0.58 and 16%. With hindsight (every case known today) the model reached 0.69. That is too weak to label named companies, so company pages list SEC cases as facts and show no fraud score.

A wider definition of failure

Small companies often stop filing instead of filing for bankruptcy. Counting a company as failed if it filed for bankruptcy or stopped filing without being bought gives 1,278 events in the test years. Faultline ranked them with an AUC of 0.84, against 0.75 for Altman Z.

Limits

Only formal bankruptcy filings count, so distressed sales and out-of-court restructurings are scored as misses. Companies that simply stop filing are not counted either way. About 40% of bankrupt companies had no score in the warning window, usually because they stopped filing or left out a needed number. Results come from one model setting that has not been tuned.
