General Electric: A warning that didn't end in bankruptcy

GE was riskier than 79-93% of filers in four of its five 10-Ks from 2016 to 2020 and lost $22 billion in 2018, but it never went bankrupt. It also had an SEC accounting case the scores missed.

Percentile of bankruptcy risk among all 10-Ks or 10-Qs (each point is one filing) filed in the 365 days up to that filing (never later ones). Shaded bands: top 5% (high) and top 20% (elevated). Each point is scored by a model that had only seen outcomes known before that year.

What the scores said

  • Faultline: riskier than 97% of filers in February 2016, 92% in 2018 and 95% in 2019, the year it reported a $22.4 billion loss for 2018.
  • Then it fell: 49% in 2023 and 14% in 2025, after GE sold businesses, paid down debt and split into three companies.
  • Accounting: the SEC announced an accounting enforcement case against GE in December 2020. Faultline's restatement-risk score for GE was low the whole time (riskier than 4% of filers in 2018).

How to read this

GE counts as a false alarm in Faultline's bankruptcy test: it was in the riskiest group and did not go bankrupt within two years. That is normal. Of all companies in Faultline's riskiest 10%, only about 1 in 9 went bankrupt within two years in the test. The score ranks how fragile a company looks, it doesn't predict that any one company will fail. GE's real trouble (huge losses, heavy debt) was exactly what the score measured; management fixed it.

The lesson

High risk is not a prediction of failure. And financial ratios are poor at spotting misleading accounting: Faultline found too little signal to publish a fraud score at all (see the track record).
