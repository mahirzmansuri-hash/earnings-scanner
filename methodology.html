Methodology

Data

All numbers come from the SEC's free EDGAR XBRL data: about 39 million reported values from 15,686 companies that filed an annual report since 2009, including 10,349 that later stopped filing. A list of only today's companies would hide every failure, which makes any warning score look worse than it is.

Point in time

Each year's numbers are taken from that year's original 10-K, dated by the day it was filed. Later corrections are kept separately and never used in tests. A number that first appeared in a later filing is left out of that year.

Cleaning

Companies tag the same item in different ways. Faultline maps 21 items to their XBRL tags. When one filing reports two different revenue figures, it keeps the one where revenue minus cost of goods equals gross profit. Five companies were checked by hand against their 10-Ks (Apple, Sears, General Mills, SVB, Conagra), and all matched.

Checking the outcomes

Bankruptcies come from 8-K filings tagged Item 1.03, but filers sometimes tag the wrong item. Faultline reads the text of every bankruptcy-tagged 8-K and drops the ones that never mention a bankruptcy: 17 of 2,139 since 2009 (for example, a 2011 Duke Energy 8-K). Receipts lists every recent bankruptcy and what the score said before it.

Percentiles

"Riskier than X% of filers" compares a filing only with the filings made in the 365 days before it, never with later ones, so the comparison uses only what was public at the time.

Classic scores

Altman Z-Score (1968) uses five ratios to measure bankruptcy risk; market value comes from the public float on the 10-K cover page. Z'' (1995) swaps market value for book equity. Piotroski F-Score (2000) counts nine pass/fail health tests. Beneish M-Score (1999) combines eight ratios that tend to move before earnings manipulation is found.

Faultline models

Two gradient-boosted tree models read 46 values per company-year: every part of the classic scores, size, profitability, debt, growth, how long the 10-K took to file, recent auditor changes, late filings and amendments, and whether the 10-K uses five warning phrases (found with SEC's full-text search). The phrases did not improve the models, but they are shown on company pages. One predicts a bankruptcy filing (8-K Item 1.03) within two years, the other a restatement notice (8-K Item 4.02). For filings made in a given year, each model is trained only on companies whose two-year outcome was known before that year started.

Quarterly updates

Between 10-Ks, each company's 10-Qs are scored too. A 10-Q's income statement covers only the year so far, so Faultline builds trailing twelve months: this year so far, plus the last full year, minus the same part of last year, using only numbers public on the 10-Q's filing date. Public float comes from the latest 10-K. A separate model is trained on 10-Q rows with the same walk-forward rule.

Why this score

Each company page breaks its bankruptcy risk into ten areas: debt and financing, retained earnings, profits, cash flow, short-term cash, sales, company size, accounting patterns, the classic scores, and warning signs in filings. For each area, the same model that scored the filing scores it again with that area's values replaced by a typical company's (the median of the years the model learned from). If the risk falls, the area was pushing it up. The areas overlap, so the effects do not add up exactly. One result can look odd: larger companies sometimes get a size effect that raises risk. That is because small companies in trouble usually stop filing instead of filing for bankruptcy, so most formal bankruptcy filings in the data come from larger companies.

What the scores are not

A score is a ranking of how a company's filings compare with the filings of companies that later failed or restated. It is not a forecast for any single company and not investment advice. Financial companies are not scored.
